On Asking Better Questions

Author Christopher Klein
Published April 10, 2026 Updated April 10, 2026
Research Writing Reflection

Overview

A good question is harder to write than a good answer. This is something I notice constantly in user research, in writing, and in conversation — and it took me an embarrassingly long time to pay deliberate attention to it.

The Framing Problem

Most questions carry their answer with them. Not explicitly — the phrasing doesn't give it away directly — but the frame of the question tells you what kind of answer is expected, what would count as a valid response, and what's been ruled out before anyone opens their mouth.

In user research this matters enormously. A question like "what do you find frustrating about X?" is not neutral. It presupposes frustration exists, it focuses attention on negative experience, and it invites complaints rather than observations. That's sometimes exactly what you want. But it's worth deciding that deliberately rather than by default.

In Research

The questions I've found most useful in research sessions tend to be the most open and the most boring. "What happened next?" covers a lot of ground. So does "Can you say more about that?" Neither of these is clever. They don't show off any particular insight. They just keep the participant talking and keep me out of their way.

In Writing

The same principle applies to writing. The most productive writing sessions I've had started from a question I genuinely didn't know the answer to. The least productive ones started from a conclusion I'd already reached and was trying to support.

What I've Changed

I've started writing my research questions down before sessions and then rewriting each one twice — once to make it more open, once to make it more specific. Neither version is always better. But the exercise forces me to notice what I'm actually asking, which is the part I used to skip.
