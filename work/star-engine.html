Overview

In 2021, I transitioned from an embedded Senior QA to the sole UX Designer within the Core Technology Group (CTG) at Cloud Imperium Games. I was responsible for a few different initiatives within the CTG, however I was primarily focused on the Star Engine Editor - which is the software interface and tools that allow over 800+ developers globally to develop Star Citizen, a massive Singleplayer and MMO space game.

Over the course of 3 years, I stood up user research foundations, documented technical issues and workflows, while also updating existing tools and crafting new ones to solve developer experiences. I used my voice to echo developer commentary when they encountered bugs or had frictions with their tools and I would then synthesize those issues into action points as best I could with my team to fit their specific needs while doing our best to afford general developers.

In 2023, my time - as well as a bunch more awesome people’s time - was cut short due the Los Angeles office shut down. I wasn't able to accomplish all of the things I wanted, but I am so happy to have established a UX practice where there was none and contributed some great tools with great people.

Looking back, I will note that I was one UX person within a Tech Group that needed at least two or more Senior UX devs on the team alongside me. That being said, I’m again grateful to have been given the opportunity and I give great thanks to my producers for being my rocks and my trusty Games User Research Book, my external mentor, and the folks in the Toolsmiths Discord channel.

On this page, I will not go into great detail about all of the individual projects, but rather condense my 3 years on the team, my struggles, team wins, and some specific project musings.

What is the Star Engine

The Star Engine is a game engine utilized by Cloud Imperium Games to create their two main games: “Squadron 42”, the Singleplayer title - and “Star Citizen: Persistent Universe” - their multiplayer title.

If you’re interested in more ditty gritty detail, You can read more on this wiki or go to any of the Citizen Con Tech talks available on youtube.

I do also highly recommend you watch a video or play Star Citizen’s Persistent Universe for yourself to understand the scope of the engines capabilities and the near-seamless traversal from surface to planet via spaceship. It’s pretty cool

The Editor - which was my aforementioned primary focus - is a software interface wrapping the game engine allowing developers to manipulate the game's features, create levels, its lighting, cinematics, animations, among many of the other pipelines used to create games. .

Foundations and UX Maturity

Much of my early days were spent modifying my US Pacific Time sleep schedule to adapt to communicating with my new teammates across the pond in GMT.

The locations under Cloud Imperium Games (CIG) at the time were LA, Austin and Montreal in North America, with a team in Frankfurt, Germany, and the home base for the largest developer population in Manchester, UK.

I was interviewing developers from all studios via Microsoft Teams and would be tasked weekly with meeting team leads and their associated members within the Core Technology Group.

Those meetings were not only used to meet and greet with developers I had never interacted with before - but also for about 30 minutes to a gracious 1 hour if a developer was chatty enough - I would gather information about the tools they used, their current deliverables, and the issues they faced as a team as it pertained to the Editor.

At the beginning I was doing my best to translate much of what I used for playtesting games, while working towards figuring out how to get from Level 2 UX Maturity - “Have a go” - to Level 4 Maturity - “Useful UX” according to the Games User Research Book. However, this wasn’t for games, this was for the tools that made games.

The best I could do at first, was to be present, listen, learn and document my observations and present them to my team. That’s what I did, and overtime it grew in a more formal process.

My User Research Setup:

  • Meeting Invite and Acceptance via Outlook and a DM via Teams
  • Meet and Greet to establish major talking points
  • Screen Share and Record via OBS
  • Transcribe using Otter
  • Develop Surveys if I needed Quantitative Data
  • Synthesize my notes into reports
  • Chat with my team about the issues
  • Make action points via Jira Tasks

These meetings would lay the foundation of the Editor team's notoriety among the other studios, eventually garnering enough of an interest among developers to create a feedback loop that the Editor and the Core Tech team never had for our global developers.

Overtime, the Editor team implemented the following Feedback Loop:

  • Interview and Survey Developers regularly, but not too much! People are busy!
  • I audited the Help Menu, which was barren and had outdated reference documentation
  • I created an Editor Confluence page to replace the outdated docs and served as a HUB
  • We created a Teams chat channel for direct Editor Support
  • We created a Patch Notes Archive and Published Patch Notes regularly
  • We also created a Patch Notes modal that appeared on startup after updating to latest
  • We put a link to Patch Notes within the Help Menu
  • We added ‘information’ buttons to all major tools, which linked to documentation!

Results:

  • Developer engagement increased!
  • Awareness increased!
  • Reports of issues increased - which rarely ever happened before because developers never reported issues!
  • Editor stability increased!
  • We reduced New Developer On-Boarding Times!
  • We increased tool fluency even among veteran developers!

A dig through an old toolbox

The Star Engine is - in my personal opinion - technically one of the most impressive game engines on the planet. The Editor Interface however - was notably archaic and crudely mapped due to untidy modification over the years; especially when compared to modern game engine interfaces like Unreal, Unity and Godot. I was hired to not only aim to improve the interface. While I never got to implement a new aesthetic to the Editor, we made progress on fundamentals by tackling many of the subtle nuances of the developer user experience within the interface.

Most any time a new hire came through the office - this engine was quite foreign to them compared to the aforementioned industry standard engines - and would need help acclimating. Acclimation to any new tool takes time for new users but also takes time away from veteran users who are pulled away from their work to explain the nuances of a workflow or specific tool to those new hires.

From duplicate toolbars, differing icon sizes, redundant or deprecated settings, and the ever present hindrance of text not scaling with different monitor resolutions - these were the long standing “deaths by a thousand cuts” issues which loomed over veteran and new developer workflows on the daily.

Luckily they hired a person who loves a good audit and documenting everything to help his team! It was foundational to many quality of life improvements we gained. So that is what I did.

All of the notes compiled from my meetings with developers over the years, my individual exploration, and my engineers legacy knowledge helped us to audit the disconnected parts of the editor and lead to overhauls of major and minor systems. Below are some examples of important growth moments for myself and the Editor team.

RASTAR BASE BUILDER

Near the beginning of my joining of the Core Tech Group, I would be asked to join a prototyping team for a Base Building tool, referred to as RASTAR. It was meant to speed up the Level Design and Environments workflow for constructing spaces on planets throughout the Persistent Universe. I was responsible for both concept design and UI implementation using their internal tool called, Dataforge. This tool allows developers to create many of our in-game classes and game objects as well as our interfaces.

As much fun as I was having working on a base building tool - I became overwhelmed and it was weighing on me that I was assisting with implementation on this project as well as maintaining existing Editor Team duties. Of which were arguably more important and the primary reason I was hired in the first place. My implementation was also poor. I was creating new components by scratch and not utilizing component libraries established by the UI team.

And so I left the project after discussing my issues with my team lead and serves as a lesson for myself to know when to say ‘no’ to a project even if it’s interesting.

There was however some valuable data gained for the UI and Editor team:

Our Editor Viewport - which primarily acts as a 3D render window of the level a developer has loaded - was not really performant with the interface setup we had as well as some other UI interactions for the player controller.

During development, the UI team and I would notice even in sterile test levels - the UI when used would lose fps during specific interactions. I would report these issues and provide performance traces. We were able to find resolution scaling and specific mouse and keybinding behavior bugs and fix those to make the tool and other play-in-viewport experiences better for developers.

EDITOR SETTINGS REFACTOR AND A NEW VIEWPORT VISUAL MANAGER

As I was meeting with teams and auditing the editor - I would note different workflows as well as developer struggles with display settings with the Viewport. Below I note major issues and what we did to tackle these issues.

Major Issue 1: Settings

The Editor Settings were all over the place and I mean that very literally. From duplicate shortcuts with different labels to buttons that had no functionality at all.

Most developers also relied on json files full of debug commands they found over the years and would pass onto one another which would be read at editor launch to display specific debug visualizations for their workflow would appear in the Viewport.

These commands would also sometimes cause developers to skip crucial Editor Prompts which would have aided them and submit changes that broke builds or tanked performance.

Json files filled with commands being passed down to teams via lead legacy knowledge that was not archived in any documentation in confluence let alone in the Editor.

Major Issue 2: Performance

Our Single-player and Multiplayer Levels, and Tech Zoo’s are populated with hundreds, sometimes thousands of objects. Each object in the engine receives visual “helpers” which could be Toggled (Show/Hide).

The viewport will get cluttered easily and developers lose focus of what matters and as a result of drawing a bunch of endless helpers they could not control the visualization of and performance would drop.

What did we do?

Using my audit of the entire Editor Settings: from scaling, text, and viewport visualizer options - we verified every menu and sub-menu option and labeled them as functional, redundant, and compiled a list for refactoring.

We also assessed a variety of workflow json files and the commands within as we wanted to see the general commands used by most teams, as well as some more obscure commands which we could surface and support within the Editor Settings.

And finally we created a new searchable window which houses all Viewport Visualization options at the top of the viewport which runs parallel with our new Editor Settings Menu.

Results

This was a win for all developers, as they could easily find, filter, and manage what is currently displayed in their Viewport.

We increased FPS by refactoring the ways we rendered specific visualization helpers, and created methods to control render distance for the Viewport in General, as well as Helpers.

And because sharing is caring - we thought it useful for developers to be able to create and share “Presets” configuration json files with one another.

A user can create multiple visual environments, rather than a singular one they have to manage each time they need to change a workflow.

For Example: We have two developers from Cinematics. One needs to set up framing for different shots - and another needs to ensure framerates are consistent throughout.

Each developer can set cinematic presets, name them, share them, and the user can toggle between the two.

Object References

At this point in time, the team's notoriety had grown and we received a message from the Single-player Level Design Team from our team's Technical Director. They actually had prepared a pitch for a tool, and had gathered a large chunk of the information and provided example screenshots and Levels for us to test with.

One of the most significant boondoggles a game developer can get into is data corruption. This can happen in a few ways from Bit rot, truncation, scheme mismatches, buffer overflows, maybe some race conditions or Asset errors. How each engine or an engine editor handles those situations is a hit or miss when it comes to providing enough detail to the user to understand what caused the issue to appear.

For this particular project story we were dealing with Broken / Missing References which were causing levels in the Singleplayer to crash immediately with Null Reference Exceptions.

Are you sure this is important? Yes or No

In the Editor, for like 10 odd years - when users pressed the Delete key or right clicked to Delete a selection of game objects - the Delete Modal - which commonly prompts a user with “Are you sure you want to Delete (Big Red Square)? - Yes / No” - did not inform the user that the Big Red Square contains a Reference to another game object.

It was one of those situations where we all sat back in our chairs and felt a mix of anxiety and comedic surprise.

“How have we managed for this long?” and “Why are we laughing about this?” which ended with us all agreeing we need this prioritized.

As we sat in disbelief that no developer or team prior took action on this prior - we got to work straight away on the design and implementing based on the specifications presented by the Single Player Team.

What did we do?

  • Developers we worked with had a very detailed breakdown of the problem and what they wanted in an interface as well as some core features.
  • With my team, I surfaced the remainder of the user flows pertaining to the Delete interactions, making sure we have “safety checks” in key areas.
  • We modified the existing modal and finally created a new window which would present itself if there any objects had references, and presented options for resolution.
  • Users can select and modify the Objects in the pending list, make their change, and go on to their next task.

As a result of this new window being implemented, our group of developers we worked with called it, “a godsend” and level decay decreased drastically once our tool branched into other streams.

This project, I do believe was successful in part for two reasons:

  • We had been successful with other projects during my time there, and were public about our desires to tackle problems for developers all over the globe.
  • Because of this, the developers sought out the correct channels, and had a plan in motion already to tackle the issues they faced, which reduced the time it would usually take to implement.

All in all, a very memorable project.

Conclusion

While I could go into some others like a new color picker, a content browser, and some layer management tools improvements - the above hold the most significance for me when it comes to my work during that time.
