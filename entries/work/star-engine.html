Overview

In 2021, I transitioned from an embedded Senior QA to the sole UX Designer within the Core Technology Group (CTG) at Cloud Imperium Games in Los Angeles. I was responsible for a few different initiatives, however my primary focus was within the Star Engine Editor - which is the software interface that contains the tools which allow over 800+ developers globally to develop Star Citizen, a massive Singleplayer and MMO space game.

Over the course of 3 years, I stood up the groups user research foundations, documented technical issues within workflows, updating existing tools, and lastly crafting new tools. I used my voice to echo developer commentary when they encountered bugs or had frictions with their tools and I would then synthesize those issues into action points as best I could with my team to fit their specific needs while doing our best to afford general developers.

On this page, I will detail a few selected projects, span my 3 years on the team. Our struggles, our wins, and some specific musings.

What is the Star Engine

The Star Engine is the game engine utilized by Cloud Imperium Games to create their two main titles: “Squadron 42”, their Singleplayer title - and “Star Citizen” - their massive multiplayer title.

If you're interested in more detail about the engine, you can read more on the Star Engine wiki page or go to any of the tech talks available on YouTube.

Star Engine Editor

The Star Engine Editor was my primary focus - is a software interface wrapping the game engine allowing developers to manipulate the game's features, create levels, its lighting, cinematics, animations and more for the two titles.

While the Star Engine is one of the most impressive game engines on the planet, the interface for the Editor is notably archaic in comparison. Untidy modification over the years has left it behind especially when compared to modern game engine interfaces like Unreal, Unity and Godot.

While I had spent some of my spare time conceptualizing an updated aesthetic to the interface - we sadly did not get to implement them. My focus throughout was primarily in specific tools. The overhaul would have taken considerable time away from core tools and fixes.

Below are some of my concepts

UX Maturity

I transitioned to this role at the beginning of the Covid-19 global pandemic. I was fortunate to be able to work from home and to be alive.

With my team largly based in Germany - my team integrated me into their end of day meetings and now that I didn't need to commute, I could stay up later to meet developers across the pond in their early free hours. Exhausting as that was sometimes, it was worth it just to meet new people and learn about their issues.

As I had no UX Lead, I was going to need external help. I applied for a mentorship program on the Games User Research site, got a mentor, picked a copy of the Games User Research, and made use of a friendly cross-tech tools-oriented developer community - 'The Toolsmiths'.

Chapter 5 of the Games User Research Book, titled, "User experience maturity levels - evaluating and improving game user research practices" Graham Mcallister details maturity levels of organizations and provides criteria to help define where you are and where you can go based on your resources.

With my hiring, the Core Tech Group had gone from Level 1: Do not do it - to Level 2: 'Have a go'.

My goal based on resources, was to get us to Level 4: Internal UX (effective) and I believe during my time I managed to do that.

At the beginning I was doing my best to translate much of what I used for QA playtests, while leveraging my available tech stack.

Tasked weekly with meeting team leads and their associated members within the Core Technology Group, I was interviewing developers from all studios via Microsoft Teams, and making notes in Confluence and Miro.

For about 30 minutes to sometimes a gracious 1 hour if a developer was chatty enough - I would gather information about the tools they used, their current deliverables, and the issues they faced as a team as it pertained to the Editor.

That first year was filled with many ups and downs. I did my best to be present, listen, learn and document my observations and present them to my team. That’s what I did, and overtime I built trust amongst the developers as the team tackled their issues, and I grew a more formal process.

Our Internal Setup:

  • Production provides UX with a Task with associated Tool and Power Users
  • UX sends a meeting invite to the point of contant developer
  • Meet and Greet to establish major talking points
  • Screen Share and Record workflow via OBS
  • Transcribe audio using Otter
  • Synthesize my notes into reports
  • Chat with my team about the issues
  • Make action points via Jira Tasks
  • Send Surveys if there is a need Quantitative Data

These meetings and projects provide the Editor team notoriety among the other internal studios, eventually garnering enough of an interest among developers where we saw tons of developers wanting to participate.

Fixing Communication

Most any time a new hire came through the office, many would struggle adapting. This editor is quite alien to many of the younger and even older generations of developers who come from years with Unity, Unreal, or even Godot which offer tons of up to date docs and support.

Internal Star Engine Editor documentation was sparse and the documentation we had, was team specific documentation. Some archives were better than others, but most were decentralized in their specific team's corner of confluence.

Another much to common feeling among developers I gathered in interviews, was the lack of communication with the Editor Team. They felt cutoff and didn't know who to contact. The Editor Team was a small aperatus within the Core Technology Group and no one was really the public point of contact.

Worse than that - if they encountered bugs, crashes, or UX issues - they rarely reported, or recorded it. And that was made worse because there wasn't any obvious method to communicate these issues to the Editor Team's Engineers and QA.

I saw the opportunity to create a much needed communication loop for developers:

  • I created a centralized Editor HUB for general use, documentation, updates, troubleshooting guides, etc
  • We created a Teams Channel for direct Editor Support
  • We created a Patch Notes Archive and Published Patch Notes regularly
  • Integrated a Patch Notes modal that appears on startup after updating to latest
  • We put a link to all of these within the Help Menu of the Editor
  • And later, a UX Requirement for every tool would add a ‘information’ buttons visible in a tool modal linked to documentation!

Outcome

  • Developer engagement increased as I became a bit of a spokesperson.
  • Reports of issues increased in the Teams Channel, QA reproduced the issues, engineering fixed more issues, and stability increased.
  • New Developer on-boarding was improved, and we even increased tool fluency among veteran developers by surfacing old but useful functionalities.
  • Developers felt like they were finally heard.

The importance of assuring a solid communication loop was the single most important aspect of my time there.

Base Builder Ups and Downs

Near the beginning of my joining of the Core Tech Group, I would be asked to join a prototyping team for a Base Building tool, referred to as RASTAR. It was meant to speed up the Level Design and Environments workflow for constructing spaces on planets throughout the Persistent Universe. I was responsible for both concept design and UI implementation using their internal tool called, Dataforge. This tool allows developers to create the many of our in-game classes and game objects as well as our interfaces.

As much fun as I was having working on a base building tool - I became overwhelmed and it was weighing on me that I was assisting with implementation on this project as well as maintaining existing Editor Team duties. Of which were arguably more important and the primary reason I was hired in the first place. My implementation was also poor. I was creating new components by scratch and not utilizing component libraries established by the UI team.

I left the project after discussing my issues with my team lead and serves as a lesson for myself to know when to say ‘no’ to a project even if it’s interesting.

Outcome

There was however some valuable data gained for the UI and Editor team:

Our Editor Viewport - which primarily acts as a 3D render window of the level a developer has loaded - was not really performant with the interface setup we had as well as some other UI interactions for the player controller.

During development, the UI team and I would notice even in sterile test levels - the UI when used would lose fps during specific interactions. I would report these issues and provide performance traces. We were able to find resolution scaling and specific mouse and keybinding behavior bugs and fix those to make the tool and other play-in-viewport experiences better for developers.

Editor Settings Refactor

As I was auditing the editor - I would note different workflows as well as developer struggles with display settings with the Viewport. Below I note major issues and what we did to tackle these issues.

Decentralized Settings

The Editor Settings were all over the place and I mean that very literally. From duplicate shortcuts with different labels to buttons that had no functionality at all in the literal settings menu. No tooltips, descriptions. It was a mess.

Most developers also relied on json files full of debug commands they found over the years and would pass onto one another which would be read at editor launch to display specific debug visualizations for their workflow would appear in the Viewport.

These commands would also sometimes cause developers to skip crucial Editor Prompts which would have aided them and submit changes that broke builds or tanked performance.

Json files filled with commands being passed down to teams via lead legacy knowledge that was not archived in any documentation in confluence let alone in the Editor. So during interviews, I would collect them and archive commands, and if possible, I would detail what each did, and if there were bugs, or perhaps something we could do to surface these functionalities for general use.

Performance

Our Single-player and Multiplayer Levels, and Tech Zoo’s are populated with hundreds, sometimes thousands of objects. Each object in the engine receives visual “helpers” which could be Toggled (Show/Hide).

The viewport will get cluttered easily and developers lose focus of what matters and as a result of drawing a bunch of endless helpers they could not control the visualization of and performance would drop.

Using my audit of the entire Editor Settings: from scaling, text, and viewport visualizer options - we verified every menu and sub-menu option and labeled them as functional, redundant, and compiled a list for refactoring.

We also assessed a variety of workflow json files and the commands within as we wanted to see the general commands used by most teams, as well as some more obscure commands which we could surface and support within the Editor Settings.

And finally we created a new searchable window which houses all Viewport Visualization options at the top of the viewport which runs parallel with our new Editor Settings Menu.

Outcome

This was a win for all developers, as they could easily find, filter, and manage what is currently displayed in their Viewport.

  • We improved general performance by refactoring the ways we rendered specific visualization helpers
  • We improved the surfacing of methods to control render distance for general and specific helpers
  • And because sharing is caring - we thought it useful for developers to be able to create and share “Presets” configuration json files with one another.
  • A user can create multiple visual environments, rather than a singular one they have to manage each time they need to change a workflow.

For Example: We have two developers from Cinematics. One needs to set up framing for different shots - and another needs to ensure framerates are consistent throughout.

Each developer can set cinematic presets, name them, share them, and the user can toggle between the two.

Object References

At this point in time, the team's notoriety had grown and we received a message from the Single-player Level Design Team from our team's Technical Director. They actually had prepared a pitch for a tool, and had gathered a large chunk of the information and provided example screenshots and Levels for us to test with.

One of the most significant boondoggles a game developer can get into is data corruption. This can happen in a few ways from Bit rot, truncation, scheme mismatches, buffer overflows, maybe some race conditions or asset errors. How each engine or an engine editor handles those situations is a hit or miss when it comes to providing enough detail to the user to understand what caused the issue to appear.

For this particular project story we were dealing with Broken / Missing References which were causing levels in the Singleplayer to crash immediately with Null Reference Exceptions, resulting in developers wasting time investigating and fixing a previous fix.

Delete this? Y/N

In the Editor, for like 10 odd years - when users pressed the Delete key or right clicked to Delete a selection of game objects - the Delete Modal - which commonly prompts a user with “Are you sure you want to Delete (Big Red Square)? - Yes / No” - did not inform the user that the Big Red Square contains a reference to another game object.

It was one of those situations where we all sat back in our chairs and felt a mix of anxiety and comedic surprise.

“How have we managed for this long?” and “Why are we laughing about this?”

which ended with us all agreeing this was high priority. We got to work straight away on the design and implementing based on the specifications presented by the Single Player Team.

Outcome

  • Developers we worked with had a very detailed breakdown of the problem and what they wanted in an interface as well as some core features.
  • With my team, I surfaced the remainder of the user flows pertaining to the Delete interactions, making sure we have “safety checks” in key areas.
  • We modified the existing modal and finally created a new window which would present itself if there any objects had references, and presented options for resolution.
  • Users can select and modify the Objects in the pending list, make their change, and go on to their next task.

As a result of this new window being implemented, our group of developers we worked with called it, “a godsend” and level decay decreased drastically once our tool branched into other streams.

This project, I do believe was successful in part for two reasons:

  • We had been successful with other projects during my time there, and were public about our desires to tackle problems for developers all over the globe.
  • Because of this, the developers sought out the correct channels, and had a plan in motion already to tackle the issues they faced, which reduced the time it would usually take to implement.

All in all, a very memorable project.

Conclusion

For Three years, I worked with a great team who supported and trusted me with an entire editor and global team pipeline. Unsure how many first time UX roles get to do that, but what a ride it was.

Being the sole UX developer was difficult, but it was made less difficult due to crucial internal and external support. I am grateful to my senior tech leadership for giving me a fantastic opportunity, teammates, and producers who were my support pillars throughout my time.
