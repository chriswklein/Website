Sharing and Caring for Your Game, Part 1: Rewards

Game Development Quality Assurance User Experience
Published October 04, 2026
Updated {Month DD, YYYY}

Sharing is Caring

Recently, someone asked me about an experience that became a pillar of how I work as a developer. As I’ve been fortunate to learn many lessons throughout my career, I responded with the story below, about my final year at University. After regailing the tale, I figured it might be useful to write and share the core pillar of my game design brain:

"Sharing is caring - both for your game and your team. Share your game early and share it often throughout development. Take feedback seriously."

— Christopher Klein, and dozens of good developers.

Traveling Back in Time

Back in 2013, Twitter was still cool and full of game developers who shared their projects openly and with humility. I was in my senior year at DePaul University in Chicago, and my classmates and I were building Tessallation, a time-travel puzzle game set in a school, starring a student whose head was a colorful puzzle cube.

We had just finished our vertical slice, an early version of the game that shows its core visual identity and gameplay, and showed it off to fellow students, faculty, and members of the public at an open event. The feedback was mixed but mostly positive!

Next up was an opportunity to take a trip to San Francisco and attend the Game Developers Conference. We cut a trailer, put together a build with some fixes from the event, and flew out.

During the conference, we managed to garner some attention and befriended the folks at Asteroid Base, who were working on Lovers in a Dangerous Spacetime, a fantastic co-op game you should play.

After the conference, we stayed in touch over Twitter. We picked up traction online, as PC Gamer had written about us. With the positive energy flowing, we prepared a new build to send over.

As a small teaser, I decided to share a screenshot from our latest build early and received a reply soon after.

The response was not what I expected.

“A targeting reticule and schoolyard? Hmm… :)” they wrote.

If the problem isn’t obvious, dear reader: a crosshair is for aiming a gun, and it’s common in games with guns to use a cross to center your aim.

Here’s the funny part. While digging through old screenshots for this post, I found that our vertical slice actually used a circle. Somewhere after that, we started trying out a crosshair, and none of us thought twice about it. I honestly don’t remember why we made the switch. Memory is fickle; old screenshots aren’t.

No one around us had flagged the change. Then, by chance, a screenshot sent to a new friend revealed how one small UI element could make our lighthearted game look sinister.

The game contains no violence of any kind, with the exception of a rough game of basketball against a sentient refrigerator… but the last thing we wanted was any hint of violence, let alone for the game to look like a school shooter game.

I was ready to puke from embarrassment after reading their response. Instead of spewing my dinner all over my keyboard, I messaged the team right away, and we went back to the circle. It never left again.

I don’t have my reply archived, but I thanked our friend a few times after sending the updated build and purchased Lovers in a Dangerous Spacetime multiple times on different platforms.

Why Sharing Paid Off

What if we hadn’t shared the game? What if we’d never met the Asteroid Base team? Would we have caught that problem, or others like it?

Any detail in your game, even a small UI element, can change how people read it. The goal of sharing early isn’t to please everyone. It’s to find out how players perceive your game while you can still adjust it to match what you intend. Your game reflects on you, and the changes you make before release matter.

Sharing shaped Tessallation in ways we couldn’t have managed on our own:

  • Our tutorial level was overly scripted. After the vertical slice feedback, we changed the level design to let players explore on their own.
  • Our friends at Asteroid Base saw one screenshot and saved us from being perceived as a school shooter game.

One other change worth mentioning came from somewhere we never expected. After GDC, PC Gamer wrote an article about our characters’ “object-head” style, and the attention led to a cease-and-desist from the owners of the Rubik’s Cube brand. We redesigned the head, and the character was better for it.

I’m so glad our education was fruitful and that the game dev community we were part of was so welcoming. Because we shared Tessallation with so many people, we had the chance to fix these and other problems before release.

Tessallation was later a finalist for Best Student Project at the 2013 Unity Awards, held at Unity’s Unite 2013 conference in Vancouver, B.C. A few of us even got to meet David Helgason, one of the founders of Unity Technologies, which was quite cool.

While we didn’t win, getting there was a personal achievement, and I believe we got there because we took player feedback seriously and acted on it.

I’ve carried that lesson to every game I’ve worked on since. If you have a friend, a spouse, or a coworker who isn’t on your project, share it with them. Their fresh take is worth it. Take the feedback seriously and improve your game.

Back to the Future

It’s been 13 years since we released Tessallation, and in that time I’ve worked on a bunch of games in both Quality Assurance and User Experience. This game holds a special place in my heart and is core to my design process.

I recently booted it up to take a trip back in time and high-five my younger self. I’m happy to say it still runs well and is fully playable end to end.

I contacted the team, and we decided to give the game a new home on itch.io so it’s archived publicly. Check it out, and share it with your friends. Sharing is caring!
